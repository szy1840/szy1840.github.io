Last week I went to tiat's [the epistemologies of slop](https://www.tiat.place/slop-epistemologies). The blurb described it as "an exhibition exploring how truth, taste, and meaning are made/changing in the age of AI generative media and algorithmic abundance." I did not enjoy it, which is why I am writing this piece, as an attempt to work out the relationship between art, beauty, and thought.

I think the greatest value of art is the value of its form, and that art hardly needs to carry, or does carry, any value as thought. I do not care whether the thought behind it is correct. Take a still life: a triangular arrangement of luminous grapes. What is its thought? Nobody cares. And if the painting is instead full of blood and gunsmoke? What I attend to, and attend to first, is the force of the image itself. Whether the thought behind it celebrates the grandeur of the scene or opposes war is not particularly important. Nor do I think the second painting's thought is much more elevated than the first's.

Contemporary art does not offer much value as form, yet it claims a higher value as thought. I disagree with that claim on its own terms, but let me start with form.

A representative example I saw at tiat is Slop Bowl. There is a flush toilet, with brightly colored things projected into it from directly overhead. Press the flush button and a simple animation plays, showing those colored things swept away and then reappearing. According to the placard beside it, the thought is:

> Slop disappears, only to be replaced by more slop. The work stages disposal as a fantasy, where discard (physical and digital) is carried away. In reality, it is continually recirculated, recycled, and monetized. Echoing the rhythms of scrolling and refreshing, the installation traps viewers in a loop of catharsis without resolution. Flush to continue.

Roughly: AI-generated slop online is overwhelming and impossible to get rid of.

Does the installation have any beauty? I don't think so. Another example is Dining Alone Together. A screen plays an AI-generated mukbang of someone eating Korean instant noodles. Beside it are some real instant noodles, so you can act out the experience of eating them. The claimed thought of the whole installation:

> As mukbang performances become increasingly exaggerated and optimized for engagement, the work asks whether companionship itself can become synthetic, and whether the feeling of eating together depends on who is actually on the other side of the screen.

You can see from the link above that the formal beauty of everything in the show is close to nonexistent. This may of course be subjective, but I believe my reaction and judgment match what many viewers actually feel, and I have no intention of settling the objectivity of aesthetic judgment here.

Set that aside, and the thought on display still has plenty of problems. First, there is a problem with the logic of the thought itself. Mukbang performances are getting more exaggerated; can mukbang be replaced by AI? What is the logical connection between those two claims? Why assume people watch mukbang mainly for companionship? Even from that short description I get a sense of extreme confusion, and confusion of that kind is repellent.

Second, there is the shallowness of the thought. Let me first make my definition explicit: I call something thought only when it has a complete chain of reasoning with some depth. "Anti-war" is not a thought. "AI content is flooding the internet" is not a thought either. I believe only works that use language as their medium can achieve the precision, abstraction, and depth that expressing thought requires. So fundamentally I do not think art needs to, or could, embody thought of any depth. And even if I loosened my definition and granted that this art has thought, only thought that is neither meaningful nor deep for me (and for many other viewers), the conclusion would not change.

I don't think art needs to or can embody deep thought, but I do think art can trigger associations, or make a person think about some theme. That is part of the value of its form. The problem is that a basic precondition for art achieving this is that it should at least confine the space of interpretation to some finite range. With the two examples above, if we did not already know the theme of the exhibition, my mind would be blank. And of course, if I wanted to, I could come up with a thousand entirely different "readings." That is precisely how these works fail at triggering association in the viewer.

Put another way, the value of this art as thought (really, as association) is not successfully carried by the work itself. That value lives in the placard next to the work and in the written theme of the exhibition. It is deeply ironic. At a contemporary art show like this, everyone standing there baffled, reading the placard beside the installation, is proof that the one meager thing a viewer takes away comes from language rather than from the art.

So I think this art has no value as beauty, no value as association, and no value as thought.

ps: Looking back at the opening, the word "how" is used ambiguously. Reading that sentence, I expected explanations of mechanisms; what the exhibition offered was mostly illustrations of themes. Can that really be called "how"? Maybe this is an ambiguity in English, or maybe my English is just not good enough. Language has its own ambiguity. Used well, that ambiguity is a kind of beauty, as in narrative misdirection built out of words. But it can also be reduced as far as possible with further specification. Given this usage, I can only conclude that whoever wrote the blurb was either ignoring and toying with the ambiguity of language, or else entirely unaware of it.
