When I started job hunting, I told myself it was because I wanted to understand how the real world works. Two months in, I'm certain that instinct was right. Over those two months, I came to genuinely understand what an interview is, what a vibe check is, what a behavioral question is. These were just words to me before. When you read about "BQ prep" online, the advice usually amounts to: prepare polished answers to common questions so you can pass the behavioral round. Maybe the people giving that advice already know the full picture and are just offering a shortcut. But reading those posts, I don't think you ever really understand what these words mean.

People make choices in the moment purely on instinct. But you have to go back and look at your own past — to understand yourself, to explain yourself. If you truly understand yourself and can express who you really are, you're already prepared for the non-technical part of any interview. I remember being completely lost and deeply resentful of computer science during my senior year applications. What I knew then was that LLMs would displace a massive number of white-collar workers, and software engineers would be among the first. Looking back, I think I unconsciously saw three paths: become someone who masters LLMs; get close to business and product; or just go work with your hands. At the time, the only path I could consciously articulate — the one with the lowest perceived risk — was the third: forget all this, go to Australia and study nursing. But you can't go against your own instincts. I knew I didn't actually want to be a nurse. I only "could accept it" as an idea. And underneath, I think I already knew about the other two options. So through a combination of luck and instinct, I ended up at Brown.

The point of that story is this: in any given moment, when you chose an offer or took a role, the reason you told yourself was probably only a small piece of what was actually driving you — maybe just "this offer showed up and it seemed fine." Preparing for interviews means genuinely revisiting your past, finding the things that shaped your intuitions. That's your thinking, your logic, your passion, your motivation. If you've tried to be an honest person, when you look back at your life you'll find a compelling thread. If you've spent a lot of moments betraying your own instincts, saying one thing and feeling another — it won't be as simple.

The interview itself is just: does the other person find the real you interesting? My takeaway from all these interviews is that an interview is genuinely the most efficient one-on-one way two humans can get to know each other. The work you describe, when you add "I think" and when you don't, the enthusiasm or humor or hesitation in how you bring something up, your gestures and eye contact — within thirty minutes, two people can actually know each other. This is how human society actually runs. What strikes me is that this mechanism, which I once found completely incomprehensible, I now deeply understand and believe in. At minimum, if both sides share that assumption, the interview becomes genuinely meaningful.

That's why I don't like "preparing for behavioral questions." If you know yourself well enough, you don't need to. For almost any question, you can give an answer that's consistent with who you actually are. The alternative — preparing specific story fragments for each question, each designed to showcase some good quality — is exhausting, and it's not answering as yourself. It's answering as a collection of half-true facets, and maintaining multiple versions of yourself is hard. Plus many questions don't have right answers, and the virtues you perform can end up contradicting each other. On the other side: when you're being genuinely yourself, you have energy left to actually understand the person across from you. Within those thirty minutes, even just the first few questions, you can read who they are, why they asked you that particular question, what they care about. Understand them well enough, and you start to sense what they'll ask next, what they took from your last answer, whether they're still curious about you. And that whole time, you're also choosing them: are they perceptive, are they respectful, are they arrogant or reductive? This is a mutual selection.

I've had behavioral questions I had no real answer for — questions I could only deflect. If you're bluffing, a perceptive person will notice that what you're saying isn't actually you. Afterward, I'd sit with the question — not as "BQ prep" but as an attempt to understand myself better, to find, for this particular season when interviewing is required, at least a direction that doesn't feel dishonest.

I think this is the same as doing philosophy. You have to first explain your own intuitive answers to yourself. Looking at what philosophers across history have thought is just a way to verify, to be provoked, to try to challenge what you already believe. Maybe at any given moment I can't answer a certain question — just as I can't fully answer the question of what life means — but I can still live reasonably well, because I've found an explanation that doesn't feel like a lie: *The great world lies ahead, and the lucky ones among us are given the chance to explore it.* I know this is a privileged explanation. It only explains my own situation. Maybe one day I'll have a better answer.
