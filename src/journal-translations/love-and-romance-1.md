Romance exists between me and abstract concepts. There is a romantic relationship between us, and it has a remarkably complete system of analogies (or metaphors) linking it to romance in the ordinary, social sense.

- **Originality**: a one-to-one relationship plus long-term binding plus the institution of marriage. Mainstream human society currently grants a creator ownership over ideas, words, and results, in the same way it endorses monogamous marriage.
- **A personal motto**: the recognition, the form of address, the "official status" that people want inside a romantic relationship.
- ...

The interaction theory of metaphor: a metaphor makes two conceptual systems interact. Each of the two objects in a metaphor carries a whole set of associations, properties, and default understandings; when they are placed side by side, the structure of one system is projected onto the other, producing a new understanding.

The act of discovering that relationship is, as above, a purely romantic moment.

## The conversation

**Me**: I've been thinking about my own definition of "romance." I find it fairly easy to get a romantic feeling out of abstract concepts. There are moments when language, or logic, or an idea like "freedom" strikes me as deeply romantic. With actual people (including fictional ones, say Ai Haibara), even someone beautiful, when I imagine her appearing in my life, what I picture isn't passion or candlelit dinners. I picture her existing in my life, us talking, that sort of thing. It feels good. Though honestly I've never imagined it in much detail.

**Friend**: That counts as a fairly pure kind of love, to me.

**Friend**: Same for me. I find it easier to feel romance toward "meaning." I feel romance about abstract relations between people, but not about two concrete things.

**Friend**: But that's only one variety. I feel romance in plenty of moments that have nothing to do with abstract relations between people. Something like "freedom" does it for me too.

**Me**: Agreed. I did have genuinely romantic moments with my ex, ones that stayed with me. But maybe it works like this: the romantic relationship itself was me conforming to society's definition of a "romantic relationship," and only under that arrangement are such romantic moments permitted, or at least made easier. Or maybe the relationship is a very invisible form of commitment.

**Me**: Thinking about it more, I do believe the "romance toward concepts" is real. There are moments when I feel a strong impulse to devote my life to a concept.

**Me**: And there's the intense admiration for brilliant writing, which can shade into envy, jealousy, possessiveness. Take those quiz-style questions in *Wild Bodhisattva* (an emotion-driven 剧本杀, a scripted murder mystery game), the ones probing how much you recognize yourself in various patterns of romance between people. If you swap the object for certain concepts, in some moments my answer would be yes.

**Me**: A motto, for instance, is a romantic relationship. A commitment I make to a piece of text: I'm willing to let these words occupy my life, or run through the whole of it.

**Friend**: Though I really do rarely feel envy, jealousy, or possessiveness.

**Me**: Same here. For me it's mostly admiration.

**Friend**: I probably wouldn't feel that my relationship to a motto is one-to-one. So I have even more of an RA streak than you do.

**Me**: What's RA, research assistant?

**Friend**: Relationship anarchy.

**Friend**: My reaction is more like "wow," and then I file it away. I've always loved what Romain Rolland said about heroism, but I wouldn't let that line occupy my life.

**Me**: Neither would I. I just meant I'm trying to find as many analogies as possible. If someone else also feels a romantic relationship with concepts, they might have a motto. I mean such people probably exist; I don't have a motto myself.

**Me**: So really I have several different forms of relationship with a lot of texts. I love *Dream of the Red Chamber* and I love Wang Shuo's poems, but those aren't the same romance. You could say I'm in a romantic relationship with both, except that with *Dream of the Red Chamber* it has become something close to a committed relationship: I acknowledge how much it shaped me and I'm willing to let it run through my life. Still not a motto. A motto feels more like the analogue of marriage in the conventional sense, or of a title.

**Friend**: To be precise, I think those emotions are native to me, but suppressing them has become a reflex, to the point that it feels like I've barely had them.

**Me**: Because you see it as a power relation, something to keep under control.

**Friend**: Look, close to 100% of INFJs have strong jealousy and possessiveness. I suspect it's because they don't think it needs suppressing.

**Me**: Thinking about it, my version of "jealousy and possessiveness" seems to be this: I read a passage that says exactly what I think, but someone else created it. Or an idea I hold has already been claimed by some philosopher. In those moments there's a flash of "why."

**Me**: Although I actually object to the ownership relation between a piece of writing and its creator.

**Friend**: Oki, get it. I don't feel it either. If someone can express it for me, that's great; it means there's a person who thinks like me.

**Me**: Wait, doesn't this connect to that conversation we had about originality?

**Friend**: It does. I noticed at the time.

**Me**: My reaction was: I thought of it too, so why should it be yours to own.

**Me**: The theory is almost too well-formed.

**Friend**: Mine was "in an open-source society, why should it be yours to own." You're triggered more from the self.

**Me**: You arrived at it from politics; I got there by analogy between concepts.

**Friend**: Actually I start from the self too. I start from an ideal of mine, it's just that my ideal is a society of mutual aid.

**Friend**: I think this point reveals a lot. We both love saying "I was just about to say that."

**Friend**: Because we both got there from ourselves. Someone else might just say "you're right."

**Me**: And there's a strange feeling of dueling wits in it, predicting what the other person is about to say and responding early.

**Me**: That's the charm of language.

**Me**: It reminds me of the time I interrogated xxx with language, which was a kind of... I don't know, but it felt like I used my relationship with language to do something satisfying in the moment and unfair to language itself.

**Friend**: Language got instrumentalized. Defiled.

**Me**: Hahahahaha
